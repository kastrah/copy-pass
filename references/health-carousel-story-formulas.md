# Health Carousel Story Formulas

Adapted from the Copy Pass source frameworks for educational health carousels.
Use these as idea generators, not rigid fill-in-the-blank templates.

## Governing rules

- `Bro to Bro:` is the standalone visual series label. The sentence beneath it should sound like
  one man speaking directly to another.
- Address the reader as `you`. Do not invent an unnamed `he` to act out the lesson.
- Use complete conversational sentences. Do not use subjectless fragments or advertising
  punchlines split across multiple full stops.
- Enter the reader's existing conversation before introducing the health lesson.
- Curiosity must connect to a practical payoff.
- One golden thread must connect the opening, explanation, resolution, and CTA.
- Specificity means concrete scenes and actions, not invented medical precision.
- Never agitate fear, manufacture urgency, diagnose the reader, or imply guaranteed outcomes.

## Opening formula bank

### 1. Point of contact

**Source inspiration:** Robert Collier.

Enter a moment already happening in the reader's life.

Pattern:
`Bro to bro, when you [specific action], have you noticed [something does not add up]?`

Examples:
- `Bro to bro, does your body still feel busy after work ends?`
- `Bro to bro, why is your clinic reading higher than the one you get at home?`

Best for: work stress, white-coat readings, sleep, medication routines.

### 2. Familiar phrase with a direct question

**Source inspiration:** Collier word pictures and story openings.

Pattern:
`Bro to bro, when you say "[familiar phrase]," what are you using as proof?`

Examples:
- `Bro to bro, when you say "I'm fine," when did you last check your blood pressure?`
- `Bro to bro, if you drink every weekend, your weekly pattern still matters.`

Best for: men's emotional health, alcohol, delayed care, hustle culture.

### 3. Delayed consequence

**Source inspiration:** Caples self-interest and consequence-led curiosity.

Pattern:
`Bro to bro, does [body response] continue after [ordinary action] ends?`

Examples:
- `Bro to bro, does your heart keep racing after the argument ends?`
- `Bro to bro, have you noticed a different reading the morning after you drink?`

Best for: anger, alcohol, stress, late-night phone use.

### 4. Wrong suspect

**Source inspiration:** Copyblogger premise and myth correction.

Pattern:
`You may blame [obvious explanation], but [specific clue] may change the question.`

Examples:
- `You may blame the monitor, but the timing of the reading may explain it.`
- `You may call it insomnia, but loud snoring gives your clinician another clue.`

Best for: diagnostic distinctions without diagnosing.

### 5. Search question made visible

**Source inspiration:** Copyblogger search intent and Collier's current conversation.

Pattern:
`Bro to bro, [exact reader question]? Start with [first useful distinction].`

Examples:
- `Why is your BP higher at the clinic? Start with where it was measured.`
- `Heart racing at 3am? The time alone cannot identify the cause.`

Best for: PAA and Creator Search Insights gaps.

### 6. Specific mistake

**Source inspiration:** Caples mistake formula.

Pattern:
`The [timing/routine/measurement] mistake that changes [reading/interpretation].`

Examples:
- `The stairs-to-cuff mistake that can distort your next reading.`
- `The "one reading is enough" mistake.`

Best for: practical measurement and medication education.

### 7. Fair contrast

**Source inspiration:** Before/after, Reeves' one idea, ethical contrast.

Pattern:
`Bro to bro, [two similar things] can look alike, but they need different next steps.`

Examples:
- `White-coat and sustained hypertension can start with the same clinic number.`
- `Being alone and feeling lonely can look similar from outside.`

Best for: distinctions, myths, symptom interpretation.

### 8. Concrete analogy

**Source inspiration:** Collier word pictures and TFPI combinations.

Pattern:
`Think about how [familiar scene behaves]. Your body can respond in a similar way because [brief explanation].`

Examples:
- `A generator may stop before the room feels quiet. Your stress response can also take time to settle.`
- `Lagos traffic clears gradually, and your body may also need time to settle after stress.`

Resolve the analogy by slide 4. Never let metaphor replace medical explanation.

### 9. First-person observation

**Source inspiration:** testimonial/story form without fabricated results.

Pattern:
`I keep hearing [reader phrase], so let me ask you [direct question].`

Examples:
- `I keep hearing "my BP is only high at work," so have you checked it on ordinary days too?`
- `I keep hearing "I sleep eventually," but how often is your sleep interrupted?`

Use only as an observation, not a fake patient story or invented testimonial.

### 10. Useful instruction

**Source inspiration:** Caples how-to and command formulas.

Pattern:
`Before you [high-stakes conclusion/action], [low-risk check].`

Examples:
- `Before blaming your medication, write down when the dizziness starts.`
- `Before trusting that reading, give your body five quiet minutes.`

Best for: immediate action and safety posts.

## Narrative formula bank

### Conversation to clarity

`Familiar phrase -> contradiction -> missing distinction -> mechanism -> check -> reinterpreted phrase -> action`

### Mystery to pattern

`Odd moment -> assumed cause -> competing explanation -> useful clue -> record/test -> pattern -> professional discussion`

### Routine to consequence

`Daily routine -> hidden accumulation -> body response -> why it matters -> small interruption -> changed routine -> action`

### Myth to better rule

`Common belief -> why it feels true -> what it misses -> mechanism -> better rule -> example -> action`

### Before to after

`Old behaviour -> confusion/cost -> turning point -> new understanding -> small change -> clearer outcome -> action`

## Momentum check

Between every pair of slides, silently insert one bridge:

- `but`
- `because`
- `so`
- `which means`
- `until`
- `instead`

If no bridge fits, the slides may be adjacent facts rather than one story.

Read the hook aloud on its own. If it sounds like a narrator, headline writer, or campaign slogan
instead of a person speaking to `you`, rewrite it.

## Marketing devices not to import

Do not use these direct-response tactics in health education:

- fear agitation designed to heighten anxiety
- false scarcity or manufactured urgency
- unsupported testimonials or before/after outcomes
- certainty created through precise but unverified numbers
- identity shame
- repeated commands to tag, share, or buy
- risk reversal language that implies medical outcomes are guaranteed
